02 How I think

How I think

  1. Step back.

    This city is built from 3,200 measurements: 40 series over 80 weeks. From above, it isn’t a city at all. It’s a pattern: seasons, a trend, districts that rise together.

  2. Get close.

    Patterns are made of details. At street level every building is one value, and the avenue is time.

  3. Follow the signal.

    Most of what you see is ordinary. The work is knowing which line to follow, and not getting distracted by the rest.

  4. Find what doesn’t fit.

    One value stands several times taller than anything near it. Then the real work: check the data before calling it an event.

03 What I build

Ideas are interesting. Making them work is what matters.

Three small things I made for this page. They work: paste your own data in, change the rules, ask a different question. Nothing leaves your browser.

03.1

Understand complexity

A messy list hides its problems in plain sight. Before fixing anything, find out what’s actually in it.

Problem
A sign-up sheet that six people have typed into.
Approach
Read every column, work out what it’s meant to hold, then find what doesn’t fit.
Result
A short, ranked list of what’s worth a look, in plain sentences.

03.2

Automate the repetitive

If a fix has to be made the same way every time, a person shouldn’t be the one making it.

Problem
The same clean-up, by hand, every time the list is used.
Approach
Write each fix once as a rule and run every rule on every row. Hold back only what needs a person.
Result
Turn rules on and off and watch the list change.

The same idea, at work

95% less manual reporting effort, by automating the preparation and moving the reports into Tableau.

300+ validation rules across roughly 100–150K trade records, used to confirm the rules were accurate for audit.

03.3

Make information useful

A chart is only useful if it answers a question. So start with the question.

Problem
A year of numbers and no idea where to look.
Approach
Each question picks its own chart; the code works out the answer, and the sentence only states what the numbers show.
Result
Four questions, four honest answers, one of them a warning.
